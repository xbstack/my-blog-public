Notes from Xiaobai

The damp, chilly fog of Huaguoyuan crept into my neck like a cold snake. I pressed the spacebar on my HHKB; the crisp click echoed through the room in Building 25, slightly grating.

I remembered a Friday afternoon in 2024. The sunlight on Dawang Road in Beijing was blinding. Clutching an iced Americano that cost 28 yuan, I watched the architect across from me get called away by HR. He was wearing a faded Uniqlo plaid shirt, his shoes slightly worn out of shape. Ten minutes later, his computer screen went black. Four years of code, ten thousand hours of logic, wiped away like trash.

That day, I didn’t say a word. I just felt my lower back crack. It wasn’t anger; it was extreme fragility. We coders thought we were building systems, but we ourselves were the most fragile porcelain bottles.

This is why I obsess over Nassim Taleb’s Antifragile. I’ve read this book six times; the corners are curled, and it smells musty. Today, I’m not talking philosophy. I’m talking about how I’ve spent the last two years in Guiyang writing this “volatility-benefit algorithm” into my balance sheet and Cursor plugins.

The full text is 5700 words. If you only want the summary, scroll straight to the bottom.

1: 🧱 Physical Definition: Rejecting “Stability” as a Slow Poison

Robustness is a scam.

In distributed systems, we strive for uptime. But a rock is also robust; step on it for ten thousand years, and it remains a rock, with no evolution.

Taleb divides the world into three piles of garbage:

  1. Porcelain bowls (Fragile): Hate volatility. Shatter at the slightest shake. If you rely solely on one salary to survive, you are a porcelain bowl.
  2. Rocks (Robust): Unafraid of volatility. Shake them, and they don’t move. If you have a large savings account but zero skill evolution, you are a rock.
  3. Muscles (Antifragile): Love volatility. Don’t train, don’t grow. The more you’re tortured, the stronger you become.

My first rule of code in my local dev environment in Guiyang is: “Any logic that cannot benefit from volatility is trash.”

Those days chasing stable OKRs in office buildings were essentially pushing myself toward becoming a porcelain bowl.

2: 📊 The Barbell Strategy: My Physical Assets and Logical Distribution

Don’t bother with medium-risk investments. That’s the most embarrassing thing you can do.

Taleb suggests: be extremely safe on one side, extremely speculative on the other. Leave the middle empty.

1. The “Old Man’s Ride” Base: 90%

  • One end of my barbell is: Dividend ETFs + a self-owned home with minimal leverage.
  • Logic: This part isn’t about doubling your money; it’s about longevity. As long as I don’t die, compound interest is guaranteed.
  • The physical detail here is: my monthly mortgage is just over 3000 yuan. I genuinely don’t care if AI steals all my work one day. This psychological “redundancy” is my margin of safety.

2. The “Uncapped Upside” Gamble: 10%

I take the remaining funds and play with the wildest tech.

  • Writing MCP Servers, orchestrating multi-agent collaboration with LangGraph.
  • Logic: Even if 10% of my time rots on a hard drive, I lose nothing. But if even one project succeeds, its returns are global.
  • It’s like buying NVDA options; losing a few thousand bucks hurts, but that non-linear convexity is something a salary will never provide.

This “barbell” structure keeps my breathing steady even when facing chaos like in 2026.

Looking at those meaningless error logs, I wanted to crawl through the network cable and burn the server down.

3: ⚙️ Via Negativa: Less is More

We programmers suffer from a disease: “feature-addiction.”

System slow? Add caching. Logic messy? Add an abstraction layer.

Taleb says wisdom often comes from subtraction.

1. Code-Level “Danshari”

While refactoring XBSTACK 6.0, I removed multiple third-party dependencies from 40.

  • Why? Every dependency is a fragile anchor.
  • The issue: One library was actually loading an 200MB model on the server side. I cut it out immediately.
  • The result: The page’s first-screen response time dropped from 1.2s to 200ms.

2. Physical Circuit Breaker for Information Sources

I uninstalled all push-notification apps.

  • Environmental noise: The hum of the exhaust fan. The smell of food from the night market downstairs outside my window. These are real.
  • Algorithmic feeds: Those trending topics, those moments of instant panic—they’re fake.
  • The logic: If you spend every day processing data fed to you by algorithms, your brain develops “cognitive overfitting.”

I’d rather spend two hours studying a topographic map of Guizhou than five minutes scrolling through Weibo’s trending topics.

4 :🚧 Common Pitfalls and Risk Audits

  1. Error: Fake Convexity

    • The Phenomenon: Using 10 leverage to buy tech stocks, claiming you are seeking convex returns.
    • Physical Audit: That isn’t antifragility; that’s suicide. Once a drawdown of 10% occurs, your principal is wiped out. You lose the opportunity to “survive.”
    • Countermeasure: Antifragility requires having “redundancy to survive until the end” as a prerequisite.

  2. Error: The Curse of Over-Optimization

    • The Phenomenon: Squeezing bandwidth to the absolute limit in a futile attempt to save on server costs.
    • Physical Audit: When a traffic tsunami hits (e.g., a major influencer retweets your link), your system will instantly crash.
    • The Fix: Always keep 30% of “idle” bandwidth. That isn’t waste; it’s the insurance premium you pay for uncertainty.

5: The Convexity Effect: Finding the Physical Anchor for 2026 Year

In an old-school barbershop in Guiyang, the barber was shaving my neck with a vintage straight razor. The chill of the blade against my skin suddenly brought “convexity” to mind.

If you do something where failure results in only a small loss but success yields massive gains, that is convexity.

As a full-stack developer, I have three daily actions that exhibit convexity:

  • Writing open-source MCP tools: The cost is the creaking of my lumbar spine; the potential reward is global credibility.
  • Documenting real-world case studies: The cost is the wear and tear on my keyboard’s spacebar; the reward is long-term cognitive assets.
  • Hiking Mount Yala: The cost is 48 hours of hypoxia; the reward is a physical reset for my biological systems.

These actions share a common trait: “Losses are linear and controllable, while gains are non-linear and uncapped.”

6. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why tie technology and investing together?

A: At their core, they rely on the same logic. Code requires redundancy, assets require margins, and decisions demand antifragility. If you hardcode a configuration in your code, you will likely go all-in on a garbage stock in your investments.

Q: Can life in Guiyang really make you stronger?

A: Guiyang itself doesn’t make you stronger, but a low-cost lifestyle gives you the right to “refuse menial work.” With that right, you can then seek out high-convexity opportunities.

Q: With things so tough now, haven’t programmers already peaked?

A: Only those who obsess over robustness and “mastery”—the fragile porcelain vases—are dying off. For programmers who leverage AI as a fulcrum for antifragile evolution, this is the best of times.
